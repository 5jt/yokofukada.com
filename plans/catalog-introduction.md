## Original text (transcribed)

In my personal view, I suspect that the Maru-obi has already made it into the craft red list. These were obi worn by ordinary people, yet today no one attaches any importance to them, nor does anyone even remember them.

It may seem self-indulgent, but I planned the Re-born Maru-obi exhibition because I wanted to preserve these pieces as a reference for women's Re-born fashion.

While Maru-obi sashes are beautiful in their own right, their shapes and patterns appear outdated when displayed, lacking a sense of freshness or novelty.

What is it that attracts modern people? How, then, can the qualities of the textile be best expressed?

I tried repurposing the large 60cm x 400cm piece of fabric by making everything from bags, cushions, and interior decoration items to even hats, yet the material shows no signs of running out.

Furthermore, the pattern on a single Maru-obi is designed to be viewed from all angles, so cutting it up to make small accessories would ruin the design.
So, I hit upon the idea of repurposing the fabric into a long tapestry to make full use of it.

Incidentally, the process of repurposing a Maru-obi(a traditional double-width sash) resembles the preparation of fish. You cut the seam along the sash's length and remove the threads; then-much like preparing dried fish. you extract the inner core and open up the fabric.After wiping away any dirt from the fabric, you meticulously smooth out creases and wrinkles using an iron with a pressing cloth, then hang the fabric to dry in the shade. It is a simple yet painstaking task that requires much patience.

To complete it as a tapestry, together two obi, each with a different pattern, for the front and back, and frame the perimeter with a solid color or a subdued pattern.

In other words, three obi sashes can be used for a single tapestry. Since 20 to 25 Maru-obi tapestries are displayed at a single exhibition, more than 70 obi are required.

The most time-consuming part of the production process is not the actual machine sewing, but rather the task of selecting three sash patterns for a single tapestry. I try out dozens of different combinations before finally settling on the right ones, and even then, if the result doesn't feel quite right, I start over, again and again.

The original obi measures about 4 meters in length, but since the ceilings in typical buildings are not that high, I decided to set the tapestry's size at 2.8 meters.

When I spread the finished piece out across the floor of my home, it revealed itself to be a gorgeous, long, and beautiful silk carpet.

I imagined that the artisan who wove this obi during the Taisho era (100 years ago) must have felt the same way gazing at the finished piece, turning it over and over to admire it from every angle.

Looking up from below at the magnificent woven piece hanging far overhead, they inwardly appraise it, judging whether it was well executed or fell somewhat short. The feeling of artisans is always like that.
